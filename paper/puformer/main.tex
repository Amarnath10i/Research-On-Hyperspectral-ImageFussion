\title{Exact Observation Operators in Deep Unfolding for Hyperspectral and Multispectral Image Fusion}

\author{\thanks{E-mail: amarnath10chinu@gmail.com. Code, training logs and all result files are available at
\url{https://github.com/Amarnath10i/Research-On-Hyperspectral-ImageFussion}.}}

\markboth{Preprint}{Exact Observation Operators in Deep Unfolding for Hyperspectral and Multispectral Image Fusion}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Deep unfolding networks for hyperspectral and multispectral image fusion alternate data-consistency steps with learned priors. Inside these steps the observation operators are usually either learned or implemented with the zero padding of convolution layers, whereas benchmark data are simulated with a different border rule. We examine what changes when the network's operators are exactly those of the simulation, image border included. With exact operators the ground truth is a fixed point of every data-consistency step; with a zero-border or a learned operator it is not, and the step pushes it away by an amount that grows with the share of border pixels. We build PUFormer, an unfolded network that uses the exact blur--decimation and spectral operators and their exact adjoints, with a transposed-attention U-Net as prior. In an equal-budget benchmark, in which five published networks are retrained from their authors' code under one pipeline on Chikusei, Pavia Centre, CAVE and Harvard at scale factors from 4 to 16, PUFormer has the highest PSNR in \qone{wins-PSNR} of \qone{settings-done} settings. A module-by-module ablation with repeated seeds shows where the accuracy comes from: removing the data-consistency step costs most, whereas cross-stage memory, the type of attention and the number of stages change PSNR by at most 0.05~dB, so a one-stage model with a third of the parameters performs almost as well. At the training blur the exact operator gains only \qone{abl-seed-gain}~dB over a zero border, but it raises the agreement of the reconstruction with its inputs by about 3~dB and is the only operator model that can be replaced at test time: given the true blur, PUFormer loses at most \qone{abl-full-swaploss}~dB for blur widths it was not trained on, against \qone{abl-zeros-swaploss}~dB for a zero border and \qone{abl-learned-fixedloss}~dB for a learned operator, and a blur estimated from the test pair itself performs almost as well as the true one. Under the protocol of a recent study comparing 17 methods, PUFormer reaches 58.20~dB on Chikusei $\times$4 and 50.69~dB on Pavia Centre $\times$4. Code, training logs and all result files are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{H}{yperspectral} imagers record hundreds of narrow spectral bands but, for a fixed photon budget, at a coarse spatial resolution; multispectral imagers do the opposite. Hyperspectral and multispectral image fusion recovers a high-resolution hyperspectral image (HR-HSI) from one observation of each kind~\cite{yokoya2017review,loncan2015review,vivone2025deep}. Classical methods formulate fusion as a regularized inverse problem, using component substitution~\cite{aiazzi2007gsa}, coupled matrix factorization~\cite{yokoya2012cnmf}, subspace regularization~\cite{simoes2015hysure} or fast Sylvester-equation solvers~\cite{wei2015fuse}. Deep networks have since become the dominant approach, from convolutional reconstruction networks~\cite{zhang2021ssrnet,ran2023guidednet} to transformers~\cite{deng2023psrt,fang2024mimosst,he2024lgct,fu2025ssdt} and diffusion models~\cite{cao2024ddif,chen2026twostage,xu2026cdps,zhu2025argsdiff}.

Model-guided and unfolded networks~\cite{xie2022mhfnet,dong2021mogdcn,huang2022dhif,fang2024dunet,tian2024hmpnet,sun2025iatun} bring the observation model back into the network: each stage takes a step that reduces the mismatch between the current estimate and the two observations, and a learned module then refines the estimate. How faithfully the network reproduces the observation model has received less attention than the design of the learned module. In most of these networks the blur--decimation and spectral operators are learned convolution layers, or are implemented with the zero padding that convolution layers apply at the image border. The benchmark data, however, are simulated with a fixed blur and a different border rule. The network's operator is then not the one that produced the data, and even the ground truth fails the network's own consistency check. In simulated benchmarks this mismatch is avoidable, because the forward model is known exactly. A second difficulty concerns evaluation: reported numbers on the same dataset vary by tens of decibels across papers because of differences in sensor response, blur, data split, normalization and even metric definitions~\cite{vivone2025deep}, so improvements are only meaningful within one fully specified protocol in which all methods are run.

This paper studies the operator question in a controlled way. We use an unfolded network, PUFormer, whose data-consistency steps apply exactly the simulation operators and their exact adjoints, border included, and compare it with variants that use a zero-border or a learned operator, or no data step at all, all trained with the same budget. We then compare PUFormer with published networks that we retrain ourselves under one protocol on four datasets and three scale factors. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The role of exact operators in unfolding.} We show that with exact operators the ground truth is a fixed point of every data-consistency step, and we quantify how far a zero-border or a learned operator moves it (Proposition~\ref{prop:fixed}). Experimentally, the exact operator yields a small but consistent accuracy gain, a reconstruction that agrees better with its inputs, and the ability to change the blur at test time, with a known or an estimated kernel, at almost no cost.
\item \textbf{A module-by-module ablation}, with repeated seeds for the key rows, that identifies which parts of an unfolded network matter: the data-consistency step and the exact operator do, whereas cross-stage memory, the type of attention and the number of stages hardly do. A one-stage variant with a third of the parameters is almost as accurate.
\item \textbf{An equal-budget benchmark} in which SSR-NET, PSRT, DCT, MIMFormer and the unfolded DHIF-Net are retrained from their authors' code alongside PUFormer on Chikusei, Pavia Centre, CAVE and Harvard at $\times$4, $\times$8 and $\times$16, with one data pipeline, one training budget and one metric implementation, complemented by a comparison with the 17 methods reported in~\cite{chen2026twostage}.
\item \textbf{An analysis of the remaining errors}, showing that the Q2n shortfall on Chikusei comes from bands that the multispectral sensor does not cover and the one on Pavia Centre from dark water pixels.
\end{enumerate}
Code, training logs and all result files are publicly available.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Model-based fusion}
Component substitution methods such as GSA~\cite{aiazzi2007gsa} inject multispectral detail into the upsampled hyperspectral bands. Unmixing-based methods such as CNMF~\cite{yokoya2012cnmf} factor both observations with shared endmembers, and subspace methods such as HySure~\cite{simoes2015hysure} regularize the HR-HSI within a low-dimensional spectral subspace, whose dimension can be estimated with HySime~\cite{bioucas2008hysime}. FUSE~\cite{wei2015fuse} solves the resulting quadratic problem in closed form through a Sylvester equation. These methods use the forward model explicitly but rely on hand-crafted priors.

\subsection{Supervised deep fusion}
SSR-NET~\cite{zhang2021ssrnet} and GuidedNet~\cite{ran2023guidednet} learn a direct mapping from the two observations. Transformers model long-range spatial and spectral dependencies: PSRT~\cite{deng2023psrt} uses pyramid shuffle-and-reshuffle attention, MIMO-SST~\cite{fang2024mimosst} multi-input multi-output supervision, LGCT~\cite{he2024lgct} local--global collaboration, DSPNet~\cite{shan2023dspnet} a dual spatial--spectral pyramid, SSDT~\cite{fu2025ssdt} dilated attention, and MIMFormer~\cite{li2024mimformer}, AMSF-Net~\cite{liu2025amsf}, ASMNet~\cite{liu2025asmnet} and DPFormer~\cite{liu2026dpformer} multiscale spectral--spatial designs. Recent work also studies state-space models~\cite{peng2024fusionmamba,huang2026hdgmamba}, mixture-of-experts routing~\cite{xiao2026ramoe} and modality interaction~\cite{huo2026mian}. General image-fusion frameworks such as U2Net~\cite{peng2023u2net} and OTPNet~\cite{yu2025otpnet} have also been applied to this task.

\subsection{Model-guided and unfolded networks}
Algorithm unrolling~\cite{gregor2010lista,monga2021unrolling,zhang2018istanet} turns the iterations of an optimization method into network stages. MHF-Net~\cite{xie2022mhfnet}, MoG-DCN~\cite{dong2021mogdcn} and DHIF-Net~\cite{huang2022dhif} unfold fusion-specific solvers, MHF-Net and DHIF-Net with operators learned as convolution layers, DUNET~\cite{fang2024dunet} adds transformer priors for unregistered inputs, HMPNet~\cite{tian2024hmpnet} extends unfolding to three modalities, and an information-aware transformer-based unfolding network (ITU-Net)~\cite{sun2025iatun}, a mutual-guidance unfolding network (SMGU)~\cite{yan2025smgu}, the perceptive spectral transformer unfolding network (PSTUN)~\cite{wang2025pstun}, which also handles arbitrary scales, and the cross-domain-aware unfolding transformer CaFormer~\cite{cao2026caformer} strengthen the learned proximal steps with transformer priors. PUFormer belongs to this family, and none of its prior's components is new: passing features from one stage to the next follows memory-augmented unfolding~\cite{song2021madun}, spectral-wise attention inside an unfolded network appears in DAUHST~\cite{cai2022dauhst}, and the transposed attention is that of Restormer~\cite{zamir2022restormer}. A single unfolded network that takes the blur kernel as an input, and therefore handles kernels it was not trained on, was shown for single-image super-resolution by USRNet~\cite{zhang2020usrnet}.

\subsection{Boundary conditions}
In classical deconvolution, an image border handled differently by the model and by the data causes ringing that spreads inward, and reflexive (Neumann) boundary conditions, i.e., reflect padding, are a standard remedy~\cite{ng1999neumann,reeves2005boundary}. In unfolded fusion networks the operators are either learned or implemented with the zero padding of convolution layers, while benchmark data are simulated with other border rules; to our knowledge, the effect of this mismatch has not been examined. Our contribution is on this side: an unfolded network whose operators, border included, are exactly those of the simulation, and an ablation that separates the exact operator from a zero-border and a learned operator under the same budget.

\subsection{Generative fusion}
Diffusion models have been conditioned on the two observations (DDIF~\cite{cao2024ddif}), constrained by the observation model during sampling (CDPS~\cite{xu2026cdps}), restricted to a learned subspace (ARGS-Diff~\cite{zhu2025argsdiff}) or combined with differential attention in a two-stage conditioning scheme~\cite{chen2026twostage}. Null-space formulations~\cite{wang2023ddnm} make the data-consistency part of the reconstruction exact. The two-stage conditional diffusion model of Chen~\textit{et al.}~\cite{chen2026twostage} holds the best published Chikusei $\times$4 results in our survey and is our main reference; its comparison also includes the correlation-aware network CLSNet~\cite{yang2026clsnet} and the dual cross transformer (DCTransformer)~\cite{ma2024reciprocal}, denoted DCT in their table.

\section{Observation Model and Exact Operators}
\label{sec:model}
Let $\X\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times H\times W}$ be the HR-HSI with $B$ bands ($B=128$ for Chikusei, 92 for Pavia Centre and 31 for CAVE and Harvard). The two observations are
\begin{equation}
\YH = \D\X,\qquad \YM = \R\X,
\label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}
where $\D=\mathcal{S}\,\mathcal{K}\,\mathcal{P}$ applies, band by band, reflect padding $\mathcal{P}$ of width $p$, a valid correlation $\mathcal{K}$ with a normalized $(2p+1)\times(2p+1)$ Gaussian kernel, and decimation $\mathcal{S}$ that keeps every $s$-th pixel starting at index zero, so $\YH\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times H/s\times W/s}$. For a scale factor $s$ we use $p=s-1$ and a standard deviation of $s/2$, which at $s=4$ is the $7\times7$, $\sigma=2$ blur of~\cite{chen2026twostage}; the kernel thus grows with the factor, so that the LR-HSI stays band-limited at $\times$8 and $\times$16. The spectral operator $\R\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times B}$ integrates the spectrum with the $M=8$ WorldView-2 band responses~\cite{digitalglobe2014srf} for Chikusei, the $M=4$ IKONOS band responses~\cite{dial2003ikonos} for Pavia Centre and the $M=3$ Nikon D700 responses for CAVE and Harvard (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Because the manufacturers publish the responses only as plots together with band edges, we model each band as a flat top with logistic flanks (4~nm roll-off) that pass exactly through the published edges, at 5\% response for WorldView-2, whose edges are tabulated at that level, and at 50\% for the IKONOS band limits (blue 445--516, green 506--595, red 632--698 and near infrared 757--853~nm), and normalize every band to unit sum.

\textbf{Exact adjoint.} Every gradient step on $\|\D\X-\YH\|^2$ requires $\D^{\top}=\mathcal{P}^{\top}\mathcal{K}^{\top}\mathcal{S}^{\top}$. $\mathcal{S}^{\top}$ inserts zeros and $\mathcal{K}^{\top}$ is a full convolution; together they are a strided transposed convolution with an output padding of $s-1$. The adjoint of reflect padding folds the padded margins back onto the pixels they were copied from. For a padded row index $j\in[0,H+2p)$ the source row is $p-j$ for $j<p$, $j-p$ in the interior and $2H-2+p-j$ in the bottom margin, hence
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
(\mathcal{P}^{\top}\mathbf{g})_i={}&\mathbf{g}_{i+p}+[1\le i\le p]\,\mathbf{g}_{p-i}\\
&+[H-1-p\le i\le H-2]\,\mathbf{g}_{2H-2+p-i},
\end{split}
\label{eq:fold}
\end{equation}
applied to rows and then to columns. With~\eqref{eq:fold}, $\D$ in the network is exactly the simulation operator, and the inner-product test $\langle\D\X,\mathbf{Y}\rangle=\langle\X,\D^{\top}\mathbf{Y}\rangle$ holds to a relative error below $10^{-13}$ in double precision at every scale factor. $\R^{\top}$ is the transpose of the response matrix.

\textbf{Why the operator matters.} Let $\tilde{\D}$ denote the blur--decimation operator used inside a network, and consider the data-consistency step on the LR-HSI term,
\begin{equation}
\Z=\X'-\alpha\,\tilde{\D}^{\top}(\tilde{\D}\X'-\YH),\qquad \alpha>0,
\label{eq:gstep}
\end{equation}
applied to an estimate $\X'$. A step of this kind should leave a correct estimate unchanged.
\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:fixed}
Let $\YH=\D\X$. If $\tilde{\D}=\D$, the ground truth $\X$ is a fixed point of~\eqref{eq:gstep} for every $\alpha$. For any other $\tilde{\D}$, the step moves $\X$ by $\mathbf{b}=\alpha\,\tilde{\D}^{\top}(\tilde{\D}-\D)\X$, with $\|\mathbf{b}\|\le\alpha\,\|\tilde{\D}\|\,\|(\tilde{\D}-\D)\X\|$. If $\tilde{\D}$ differs from $\D$ only in its border rule (e.g., zero instead of reflect padding), $(\tilde{\D}-\D)\X$ is nonzero only on the LR pixels whose kernel support reaches into the padding, i.e., within $\lceil p/s\rceil$ pixels of the LR border.
\end{proposition}
\begin{IEEEproof}
With $\X'=\X$ the step gives $\Z-\X=-\alpha\,\tilde{\D}^{\top}(\tilde{\D}\X-\D\X)=-\mathbf{b}$, which vanishes when $\tilde{\D}=\D$; the bound follows from the submultiplicativity of the operator norm. Both operators compute the same valid correlation of the padded image followed by the same decimation; they differ only in the padded values, which enter an output pixel only if its $(2p+1)\times(2p+1)$ support overlaps the padding, i.e., if the output pixel lies within $\lceil p/s\rceil$ pixels of the LR border.
\end{IEEEproof}
The fraction of affected LR pixels in an $h\times w$ LR image is about $2\lceil p/s\rceil(h+w)/(hw)$, so a mismatched border is a small perturbation for a $64\times64$ LR image but not for the $16\times16$ LR patches on which networks are trained. A learned operator, in contrast, differs from $\D$ everywhere. In both cases the learned prior has to cancel $\mathbf{b}$, a bias that depends on the image content, in addition to its actual task. The same argument explains why an exact operator can be replaced at test time: if the data were produced by a different blur $\D'$, using $\tilde{\D}=\D'$ restores the fixed-point property for the new data, whereas a network that has absorbed a mismatched or learned operator into its prior cannot be corrected in this way. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} measures $\mathbf{b}$ and its consequences.

\subsection{Unfolded iterations}
PUFormer (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}) starts from the bicubic upsampling $\X_0$ of $\YH$ and performs $K=3$ stages. Stage $k$ first takes a gradient step on both data terms,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\Z_k=\X_{k-1}&-s^2\alpha_k\,\D^{\top}(\D\X_{k-1}-\YH)\\
&-\beta_k\,\R^{\top}(\R\X_{k-1}-\YM),
\end{split}
\label{eq:step}
\end{equation}
with learnable step sizes $\alpha_k=\mathrm{softplus}(a_k)$ and $\beta_k=\mathrm{softplus}(b_k)$. The factor $s^2$ compensates the $1/s^2$ gain of $\D^{\top}\D$ on smooth content under $\times s$ decimation, so that both steps start at a comparable scale. The prior then predicts a residual,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
(\Delta_k,\mathbf{F}_k)&=\mathcal{P}_k\big([\Z_k,\YM,s^2\D^{\top}(\D\Z_k-\YH)],\,\mathbf{F}_{k-1}\big),\\
\X_k&=\Z_k+\Delta_k,
\end{split}
\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
where the third input tells the prior where the updated estimate still disagrees with $\YH$, and $\mathbf{F}_{k-1}$ are the final decoder features of the previous stage (cross-stage memory, merged by a $1\times1$ convolution). The last convolution of every prior is initialized to zero, so training starts from the pure physics iteration. The image path, including all operator applications, stays in 32-bit floating point; only the priors run in 16-bit mixed precision~\cite{micikevicius2018mixed}.

\subsection{Transposed-attention prior}
Each prior is a three-level U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015unet} with widths 48, 96 and 192, two, three and four transformer blocks on the encoder levels and bottleneck, mirrored decoder levels, and two refinement blocks. A block follows Restormer~\cite{zamir2022restormer}: multi-Dconv-head transposed attention computes queries, keys and values with $1\times1$ and depth-wise $3\times3$ convolutions, normalizes each feature map over space, and forms a $c'\times c'$ attention matrix across channels per head (1, 2 and 4 heads on the three levels) with a learned temperature. Attention is thus computed between spectral feature maps rather than between spatial positions, its cost is linear in the number of pixels, and the receptive field of every block covers the whole image. A gated depth-wise feed-forward network with expansion 2.66 follows. The three priors hold 3.37--3.38~M parameters each; the whole network has 10.13~M parameters for Chikusei and \pav{params}~M for Pavia Centre, whose input and output convolutions are smaller.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{PUFormer inference}
\label{alg:puformer}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\YH$, $\YM$, exact operators $\D,\D^{\top},\R,\R^{\top}$
\State $\X_0\gets\mathrm{bicubic}_{\times s}(\YH)$, $\mathbf{F}_0\gets\varnothing$
\For{$k=1,\dots,K$}
  \State $\Z_k\gets$ data step \eqref{eq:step} \Comment{fp32}
  \State $\mathbf{r}_k\gets s^2\,\D^{\top}(\D\Z_k-\YH)$
  \State $(\Delta_k,\mathbf{F}_k)\gets\mathcal{P}_k([\Z_k,\YM,\mathbf{r}_k],\mathbf{F}_{k-1})$ \Comment{fp16}
  \State $\X_k\gets\Z_k+\Delta_k$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\X_K$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Training objective}
With ground truth $\X$, the loss is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L}={}&\|\X_K-\X\|_1+0.1\sum_{k<K}\|\X_k-\X\|_1\\
&+\lambda_{\mathrm{SAM}}\,\mathrm{SAM}(\X_K,\X)+\lambda_{\mathrm{S}}\big(1-\mathrm{SSIM}(\X_K,\X)\big),
\end{split}
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
with mean absolute errors, deep supervision of the intermediate stages, the mean spectral angle in radians, and a band-wise Gaussian SSIM~\cite{wang2004ssim} whose data range is the maximum of each training patch. We use $\lambda_{\mathrm{SAM}}=0.05$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{S}}=0.1$; both terms are computed in 32-bit precision. An exponential moving average of the weights (decay 0.999)~\cite{polyak1992averaging} is used for validation and testing.

\subsection{Self-ensemble}
At test time we average the prediction for the input with the transposed prediction for the transposed input~\cite{lim2017edsr}. Transposition preserves the decimation phase, so both passes see observations generated by exactly the same operator. Flips and rotations do not: flipping a 256-pixel row maps sampled position $4m$ to $255-4m$, which has phase three, so we do not use them.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\subsection{Data and protocol}
Chikusei~\cite{yokoya2016chikusei} is an airborne scene of $2517\times2335$ pixels with 128 bands from 363 to 1018~nm. The split sizes follow~\cite{chen2026twostage}: eight $256\times256$ test images, 64 validation patches and 832 training patches of $64\times64$. Because their locations are not specified there, we fix them as follows. We take the central $2048\times2048$ crop, which contains no no-data border, and divide it by its global maximum (15{,}133 digital numbers, DN). The test images are the eight $256\times256$ tiles of the top 256 rows, the validation patches tile rows 272--400, and training draws random $64\times64$ crops with dihedral augmentation from rows 416--2048 (800 non-overlapping patch equivalents). Sixteen-row gaps keep the three regions disjoint.

Pavia Centre~\cite{gamba2004pavia} was recorded by the ROSIS sensor over the centre of Pavia, Italy; the distributed scene has $1096\times715$ pixels at 1.3~m and 102 bands between 430 and 860~nm. For this dataset, \cite{chen2026twostage} uses the benchmark files released with PSRT~\cite{deng2023psrt}: 92 bands, a four-band multispectral image, four $256\times256$ test images and 142 validation and 1284 training patches of $64\times64$. These files are distributed through a file-sharing service that we could not access, so we rebuild the benchmark from the original scene to the same specification. We keep bands 11--102 (473--860~nm if the 102 bands are taken as evenly spaced over 430--860~nm), divide by the maximum of the retained bands (8{,}000~DN), apply the same blur and decimation as for Chikusei, and simulate the HR-MSI with the IKONOS responses. The test images are the four $256\times256$ tiles of the top-left $512\times512$ region. The 63 validation patches lie on a 32-pixel grid in rows 528--656, and training draws random $64\times64$ crops with dihedral augmentation from rows 672--1096 and from the strip to the right of the test region (rows 0--512, columns 528--715), 97 non-overlapping patch equivalents in total. Gaps of 16 pixels keep the regions disjoint. As a check on the rebuilt data, our GSA implementation reaches \pav{gsa-PSNR}~dB on our test images, against \pav{tipgsa-PSNR}~dB reported for GSA in~\cite{chen2026twostage}.

For all datasets we follow Wald's protocol~\cite{wald1997fusion}: the original image serves as ground truth and both observations are simulated from it, and every image, including every training crop, is degraded on its own with~\eqref{eq:obs}. Table~\ref{tab:protocol} summarizes the two remote-sensing protocols.

\textbf{CAVE and Harvard.} To test the method beyond airborne scenes we add the two indoor/outdoor benchmarks most used in the fusion literature. CAVE~\cite{yasuma2010cave} has 32 scenes of $512\times512$ pixels with 31 bands (400--700~nm). We use the common 20/12 split~\cite{xie2022mhfnet,ma2024reciprocal}: the first 20 scenes in alphabetical order train, and the last 12, as full $512\times512$ images, test. Harvard~\cite{chakrabarti2011harvard} has 50 images of $1040\times1392$ pixels with 31 bands (420--720~nm), split into 30 training and 20 test images; each image is divided by its own maximum, and the central $512\times512$ crop of each test image is tested. In both cases the bottom-right corner of every training image is held out for validation (80 and 60 patches of $64\times64$) and the HR-MSI is simulated with the Nikon D700 response used throughout this literature~\cite{xie2022mhfnet,ma2024reciprocal}. RMSE is reported on the 8-bit scale for these two datasets.

\textbf{Scales.} Besides $\times$4 we evaluate $\times$8 on all four datasets and $\times$16 on Chikusei and Pavia Centre, with the blur defined in Section~\ref{sec:model} ($(2s-1)\times(2s-1)$ Gaussian, standard deviation $s/2$). With a fixed $7\times7$ kernel the LR-HSI would be strongly aliased at $\times$16, and the normalization of the data step in~\eqref{eq:step} would no longer hold, because most HR pixels would not lie under any kernel. At $\times$16 a $64\times64$ patch leaves a $4\times4$ LR-HSI, so all methods train on $128\times128$ patches (batch 4) there.

\textbf{Equal-budget benchmark.} Reported numbers come from different splits, normalizations and metric code (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}), so for the main comparison we retrain every learned method ourselves under one pipeline: SSR-NET~\cite{zhang2021ssrnet}, PSRT~\cite{deng2023psrt}, DCT~\cite{ma2024reciprocal}, MIMFormer~\cite{li2024mimformer} and DHIF-Net~\cite{huang2022dhif}, built from their authors' code\footnote{PSRT: github.com/Deng-shangqi/PSRT (commit 9c2be90); DCT: github.com/qingma2016/DCTransformer (bd75b15); MIMFormer: github.com/meiruni/MIMFormer (0904158); DHIF-Net: github.com/TaoHuang95/DHIF-Net (6434366). Only the numbers of input and output bands and the scale factor are changed; DHIF-Net's code covers $\times$8 and $\times$16, and at $\times$4 we use one of the two stride-4 layers of its $\times$16 operator.}. They cover a CNN, a pyramid window transformer, the dual-cross transformer that is among the strongest entries of~\cite{chen2026twostage}, a multiscale mixer, and a model-guided unfolding network whose blur, decimation and spectral operators are learned convolution layers. Every method, PUFormer included, is trained for the same 4~h on one Tesla T4, with the same random patches and augmentation, batch size, EMA weights and validation-only model selection. The published networks keep their official optimizer settings (Adam, L1 loss, learning rate $10^{-4}$, or $3\times10^{-4}$ for DHIF-Net, no weight decay or gradient clipping) and are trained in 32-bit precision as released; DCT and MIMFormer use gradient checkpointing to fit into the 16~GB of a T4, which does not change what they compute. The test metrics are computed by the same code for all methods.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
We report the eight metrics of~\cite{chen2026twostage}, averaged over the test images: PSNR; SSIM~\cite{wang2004ssim}; the spectral angle mapper (SAM, degrees, averaged over the pixels whose ground-truth and estimated spectra are both nonzero)~\cite{yuhas1992sam}; ERGAS with the resolution ratio $s$~\cite{wald2000ergas}; Q2n~\cite{garzelli2009q2n} on $32\times32$ blocks, computed on the image in DN (on the 16-bit scale for CAVE and Harvard) exactly as in the reference implementation of the pansharpening toolbox~\cite{vivone2015critical,deng2022machine}; the band-averaged correlation coefficient (CC); the spatial correlation coefficient (SCC)~\cite{zhou1998scc} of Sobel gradient magnitudes as in the same toolbox; and RMSE in DN (on the 8-bit scale for CAVE and Harvard). The headline PSNR is $10\log_{10}(1/\mathrm{MSE})$ over the whole normalized image, i.e., its peak is the dataset maximum. The PSNR values of~\cite{chen2026twostage} also use a fixed peak: for every row of their Chikusei table, $\mathrm{RMSE}\cdot10^{\mathrm{PSNR}/20}$ is between 16.6k and 17.1k~DN. Since our peak is lower (15{,}133~DN), our headline PSNR is conservative; with a 16.7k~DN peak it would be 0.86~dB higher. For Pavia Centre the same product lies between 7.7k and 8.4k~DN (median 8.07k), close to our peak of 8{,}000~DN, so there the PSNR values are on the same scale. The SSIM definition of~\cite{chen2026twostage} is not stated. We report SSIM as in the code released with PSRT~\cite{deng2023psrt}, whose benchmark data~\cite{chen2026twostage} adopts (Gaussian $11\times11$ window, $\sigma=1.5$, data range 1, i.e., the dataset peak), and additionally a stricter variant whose data range is each image's own maximum. We also report observation consistency, $\mathrm{PSNR}(\D\hat{\X},\YH)$ and $\mathrm{PSNR}(\R\hat{\X},\YM)$.

\subsection{Implementation}
PUFormer is implemented in PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} and trained on Kaggle NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs (Table~\ref{tab:impl}). On Chikusei we train in two runs. The first run trains from scratch on one GPU with zero-padded operators and the L1 losses only. The final model (v3) fine-tunes it with the exact reflect-border operators and the full loss~\eqref{eq:loss} on two GPUs with distributed data parallelism~\cite{li2020ddp}. On Pavia Centre, a single run trains the model from scratch with the exact operators and the full loss on two GPUs, within one 12-hour session that also runs all tests. All runs use AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw} ($\beta=(0.9,0.99)$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), a 1000-iteration linear warm-up followed by a cosine decay~\cite{loshchilov2017sgdr} to $10^{-3}$ of the peak rate that ends at the time budget, and gradient-norm clipping at 0.1. The weights with the best validation PSNR are tested once. The ablation and benchmark runs (Sections~\ref{sec:ablation} and~\ref{sec:bench}) train from scratch with these settings, the full loss and a single GPU for 4~h each; their models are evaluated without self-ensemble.

\subsection{Ablation study}
\label{sec:ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} changes one module of the full model at a time and retrains each variant from scratch with the same budget (4~h on one T4, about 40{,}000 iterations for the full model). On Pavia Centre the full model and the zero-border variant were trained with three seeds; their PSNR varies by only \qone{abl-full-std} and \qone{abl-zeros-std}~dB across seeds, which sets the scale for the other differences.

\textbf{Data step and operator.} Removing the data-consistency step together with the residual input (row~(\qone{abl-nophys-row})) costs \qone{abl-nophys-absdelta}~dB and lowers the agreement of the reconstruction with the LR-HSI from \qone{abl-full-consH} to \qone{abl-nophys-consH}~dB. Keeping the exact kernel but replacing the reflect border by zero padding (row~(\qone{abl-zeros-row})) costs \qone{abl-seed-gain}~dB on average over the three seeds, and each zero-border seed is below each full-model seed. A blur kernel and spectral response learned with the network (row~(\qone{abl-learned-row})), the operator model of DHIF-Net and MHF-Net, cost \qone{abl-learned-absdelta}~dB. Both variants reproduce the LR-HSI about 3~dB worse than the exact model (\qone{abl-zeros-consH} and \qone{abl-learned-consH} against \qone{abl-full-consH}~dB). This is what Proposition~\ref{prop:fixed} predicts: their data steps move the ground truth, and the prior has to compensate. On the test images the displacement $\|\mathbf{b}\|$, relative to $\|\alpha\tilde{\D}^{\top}\tilde{\D}\X\|$, is \qone{fp-pavia-zeros-test}\% for the zero border and \qone{fp-pavia-learned-test}\% for the learned operator, and on $64\times64$ crops, where border pixels are a larger share, \qone{fp-pavia-zeros-crop}\% and \qone{fp-pavia-learned-crop}\%.

The accuracy gap at the training blur is small; it becomes large when the blur changes (last column of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:operator}). Given the true blur at test time, the exact model loses at most \qone{abl-full-swaploss}~dB for $\sigma\in\{1.5,2.5,3.0\}$, the zero-border model with the same swap \qone{abl-zeros-swaploss}~dB, and the learned operator, which cannot be replaced, \qone{abl-learned-fixedloss}~dB. Without the swap the exact model loses \qone{abl-full-fixedloss}~dB: it is the most sensitive variant to a wrong operator, and the only one that fully recovers when given the right one.

\textbf{Prior.} Cross-stage memory (row~(\qone{abl-nomem-row})), the residual input (row~(\qone{abl-nores-row})), the type of token mixer (rows~(\qone{abl-conv-row}) and~(\qone{abl-window-row})) and the number of stages (rows~(\qone{abl-k1-row})--(\qone{abl-k4-row})) change the PSNR on Pavia Centre by at most 0.05~dB, comparable to the spread across seeds. A one-stage model with \qone{abl-k1-params}~M parameters is within \qone{abl-k1-absdelta}~dB of the full model with \qone{abl-full-params}~M; part of this parity comes from the fixed time budget, in which the smaller model completes almost three times as many iterations. The SAM and SSIM loss terms (row~(\qone{abl-l1-row})) do not improve the PSNR, whereas deep supervision of the intermediate stages does (row~(\qone{abl-nodeep-row}); \qone{abl-nodeep-absdelta}~dB lower without it). We keep the full model for all other experiments, but the ablation shows that PUFormer's accuracy comes from its exact data-consistency steps rather than from its transformer prior.

The PSNR gain is larger than on Chikusei although the training region is eight times smaller (97 against 800 non-overlapping patch equivalents).

The Q2n shortfall comes from one test image, which also has the largest SAM (Table~\ref{tab:perimage}, Fig.~\ref{fig:sampavia}). On images 1, 3 and 4, Q2n is 0.9987--0.9991, at or above the best reported value, but SAM is still 1.55--1.68$^\circ$ (mean 1.60$^\circ$), above the best reported 1.454$^\circ$: the water image explains the Q2n gap, but only part of the SAM gap. Image 2 contains a stretch of the river Ticino: \pav{water-2}\% of its pixels have a positive normalized difference water index (green against near infrared), against \pav{water-1}, \pav{water-3} and \pav{water-4}\% in the other images, and its SAM is 2.72$^\circ$ and its Q2n 0.904. Water reflects little light, so its spectra are dominated by sensor noise that neither observation carries. The per-band noise that we estimate from the ground truth alone makes an angle of \pav{noise-floor-dark}$^\circ$ with the noise-free spectrum in the darkest tenth of the pixels, against \pav{noise-floor-bright}$^\circ$ in the brightest tenth. Over water PUFormer's SAM is \pav{sam-water}$^\circ$, over all other pixels \pav{sam-nowater}$^\circ$, and the darkest 10\% of the pixels account for \pav{sam-dark10}\% of its mean SAM. The same pixels make image 2 nearly flat within many $32\times32$ blocks, where Q2n divides the error by a very small local variance; image 2 alone carries 97\% of the Q2n deficit $\sum_i(1-\mathrm{Q2n}_i)$ of the four images. Whether the test images of~\cite{chen2026twostage} contain water is not stated, so on this dataset SAM and Q2n are the least comparable of the eight metrics and RMSE the most.

As on Chikusei, our GSA scores below the value reported for it (\pav{gsa-PSNR} against \pav{tipgsa-PSNR}~dB), so the rebuilt data do not favor our method. The reconstruction reproduces its inputs to within \pav{cons-H}~dB (LR-HSI) and \pav{cons-M}~dB (HR-MSI). Validation PSNR stops improving after \pav{best-it} iterations (Fig.~\ref{fig:trainpavia}), and those weights are tested; the test PSNR, logged but never used, peaks at \pav{test-peak}~dB at \pav{test-peak-it} iterations, against \pav{test-at-best}~dB for the selected weights.

\subsection{Robustness and test-time operator swap}
Published methods are usually trained and tested with the same degradation. We test the final model on inputs made with a different blur or a shifted spectral response, and with Gaussian noise added to both observations (Table~\ref{tab:robust}, Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}). Because the operators are explicit, PUFormer can also be given the true test-time operator without retraining (``swap''). With the training operator, a blur mismatch costs 3.9--7.5~dB. With the true blur swapped in, the loss is at most 0.26~dB for $\sigma$ between 1.5 and 3.0; with the zero-border operators of the first run, the same swap recovered only to 55.2--57.1~dB. A shifted spectral response is not compensated by the swap, which lowers the PSNR by a further 0.5~dB: the priors, trained with one response, rely on it beyond the data step. Noise is the main weakness of a model trained on noise-free data; PUFormer still exceeds GSA by 10.0~dB at 40~dB SNR and by 2.8~dB at 30~dB SNR. On Pavia Centre the blur behaves the same way: the training operator loses \pav{blur-fixed-loss-min}--\pav{blur-fixed-loss-max}~dB and the swapped-in true operator at most \pav{blur-swap-loss-max}~dB. Unlike on Chikusei, swapping in a shifted IKONOS response raises the PSNR slightly (by 0.2--0.3~dB), perhaps because an 8~nm shift is small relative to the 66--96~nm IKONOS bands, and the margins over GSA under noise are larger (\pav{noise40-margin}, \pav{noise35-margin} and \pav{noise30-margin}~dB at 40, 35 and 30~dB SNR).

\subsection{Operators estimated from the test pair}
\label{sec:blind}
The swap above assumes that the test blur is known. In practice it has to be estimated. Because $\D$ acts on space and $\R$ on wavelength, they commute, and for the true blur $\D\YM=\R\YH$ holds exactly. We therefore estimate the blur width of each test image as $\hat\sigma=\arg\min_{\sigma}\|\D_{\sigma}\YM-\R\YH\|^2$ by a grid search with step $0.05$, using only the two observations and the training spectral response, and give $\D_{\hat\sigma}$ to the network. Table~\ref{tab:blind} compares this with the training operator and the true one, without and with 35~dB noise on both inputs. On Pavia Centre the estimate is within \qone{blind-pavia-s3-sigerr} of the true width at $\sigma=3$, and with it the exact model's worst PSNR over all test blurs is \qone{blind-pavia-worst-exact_estimated}~dB, against \qone{blind-pavia-worst-exact_fixed}~dB with the training operator, \qone{blind-pavia-worst-zeros_estimated}~dB for the zero-border model with the same estimate, and \qone{blind-pavia-worst-learned_fixed}~dB for the learned operator. The spectral response is not estimated: a swapped response did not help even when it was the true one (Table~\ref{tab:robust}), because the priors depend on the training response beyond the data step.

\subsection{Where the Q2n gap on Chikusei comes from}
\label{sec:q2n}
Q2n normalizes every band of every $32\times32$ block by the ground truth's own mean and standard deviation in that block, so an error that is small in absolute terms counts heavily in a band that is nearly flat within a block. Table~\ref{tab:q2n} and Fig.~\ref{fig:q2n} decompose the Q2n of the final model (\QtwonVthree). If the eight ultraviolet bands (363--404~nm) were reconstructed exactly, Q2n would rise to \QtwonExactUV; if the last 24 bands were exact as well, to \QtwonExactUVNIR. In the worst 10\% of blocks, the error energy of these eight bands equals \RelErrUV\ of the ground truth's local variance, against at most \RelErrMaxOther\ for any other group of eight bands. The fine detail of these bands cannot be predicted from the inputs: a least-squares injection of the multispectral detail, fitted on the test ground truth itself, reduces the band-1 error only from \OracleBicA\ to \OracleLinA~DN, while PUFormer reaches \OracleModelA~DN, and PUFormer is more accurate than this oracle in every band we checked.
Q2n is therefore nearly insensitive to overall accuracy on these test images: blending our reconstruction toward bicubic interpolation lowers the PSNR from \CalibPsnrHi\ to \CalibPsnrMid~dB but Q2n only from \CalibQHi\ to \CalibQMid\ (Fig.~\ref{fig:q2n}, right). The values reported in~\cite{chen2026twostage} do not follow PSNR in this way. Between 52.2 and 53.6~dB they range from 0.9832 (DCT) to 0.9963 (DDIF); of the 16 methods within the PSNR range of our curve, 11 lie above it and 5 below. U2Net, for example, is listed at 0.9928 at 50.55~dB, a value our implementation reaches only near 57.6~dB. We conclude that the Q2n values of~\cite{chen2026twostage} were most likely computed with a different implementation, normalization or test region, and that they are not directly comparable with ours.

\subsection{Complexity}
PUFormer has 10.13~M parameters and needs 18.6~GFLOPs for a $64\times64$ patch and 297~GFLOPs for a $256\times256$ image. On one Tesla T4 it reconstructs a $256\times256$ image with 128 bands in 0.20~s with mixed precision (92 bands: \pav{infer}~s), and the self-ensemble doubles this. Because the attention is computed across channels, memory and time grow linearly with the number of pixels. \section{Discussion and Limitations}
\textbf{What the exact operator buys.} At the blur used for training, the exact operator improves PSNR only slightly over a zero-border or a learned operator, by less than a tenth of a decibel on Pavia Centre. Its clearer effects are a reconstruction that reproduces its inputs about 3~dB more closely, and a network whose operator can be exchanged at test time, which turns a blur mismatch from a loss of several decibels into one of about a tenth of a decibel, with the true or an estimated blur. The ablation also shows that the transformer prior is not where the accuracy comes from; a simpler prior, or a single stage, performs almost as well. We therefore see the exact operator as a property worth enforcing in any unfolded fusion network rather than as a feature of PUFormer's architecture.

\textbf{Limitations.} All experiments use simulated data, in which the operators are known up to the parameters we vary; on real sensor pairs the blur, the spectral response and the co-registration are only approximately known. The blur estimate of Section~\ref{sec:blind} handles the first, but a mismatched spectral response is not compensated by swapping it, and training with randomized responses would be needed. The models are trained without noise, which costs accuracy at low signal-to-noise ratios (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). The equal-budget benchmark gives every method the same 4~h on one GPU; this is fair in compute but favors fast networks, and although the longer-budget check and the comparison with reported values suggest that the ranking holds, longer training could narrow some gaps. The comparison with reported results in Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:pavia} rests on test images that follow the published protocol but are not necessarily the same pixels, and on Pavia Centre data rebuilt from the original scene. There RMSE in digital numbers is the most robust basis, whereas SSIM with a per-image data range, Q2n and, on Pavia Centre, SAM are below the best reported values. Finally, the test sets are small (4 to 20 images), which limits per-image significance tests.

\section{Conclusion}
We studied how exactly the observation operators inside a deep unfolding network should match the operators that generated the data. With exact operators, border included, the ground truth is a fixed point of every data-consistency step; with a zero border or a learned operator it is not. In an ablation with repeated seeds, the exact operator gives a small but consistent accuracy gain, a reconstruction that agrees better with its inputs, and a network whose blur can be replaced at test time with almost no loss, also when the blur is estimated from the test images. Most components of the transformer prior, in contrast, can be removed with little effect. In an equal-budget benchmark against five published networks retrained from their authors' code on four datasets and scale factors from 4 to 16, the resulting network, PUFormer, has the highest PSNR in \qone{wins-PSNR} of \qone{settings-done} settings, and under the protocol of~\cite{chen2026twostage} it reaches 58.20~dB on Chikusei $\times$4 and 50.69~dB on Pavia Centre $\times$4. Applying exact operators to real sensor pairs with estimated blur and spectral response is the main direction for future work. All code, training logs and result files are publicly available.
